01 — Ground rules

Start with the part that’s in your favour.

If a page opens by telling you vaping is as bad as smoking, you can close it, because that isn’t true and you already know it. So here is the strongest evidence on your side, stated as generously as it deserves.

Measured in actual people, not models: someone who only vapes carries 98% less NNAL — a marker for a tobacco-specific carcinogen — than someone who smokes. The UK government’s own review found the same pattern across a whole panel of markers, and those are the bars on the right. For a smoker, switching is a large, real, well-evidenced win. Cochrane rates it high certainty that nicotine vapes beat patches and gum for quitting cigarettes.

Here is the turn. Every one of those numbers compares vaping to smoking. None of them compares vaping to nothing. And on that second question, the same UK review — from the health authority most supportive of vaping anywhere in the world — is not ambiguous:

“…findings of higher absolute exposure from vaping compared with not using any nicotine products reinforce the need to discourage people who have never smoked from taking up vaping.” Office for Health Improvement and Disparities, Nicotine vaping in England evidence review

That is the whole argument, and the rest of this page is detail. Two different questions, two different answers. The evidence that vaping helps smokers quit is not evidence that vaping is good for you.

Vaper vs smoker, by toxicant marker

    Biomarker reductions in people who vape and don’t smoke, versus people who smoke. Real, and large. Also not the comparison that applies to you if you never smoked. — OHID evidence review

    02 — Composition

    It is not water vapour. There is no water in it.

    It is propylene glycol and glycerine, heated until they aerosolise — carrying nicotine, flavour chemicals, aldehydes made by cooking the solvent, and metal picked up from the coil on the way out.

    The last two cards are there deliberately. A page that only lists the alarming findings is a page you should stop trusting around card three.

    03 — Dependence

    Why it got hard to stop so fast.

    Because it was engineered to be. Nicotine salts removed the harshness that used to cap how much anyone could take in one go. The device in your pocket is not beating your willpower — it is doing exactly what it was designed to do, to a person whose brain works normally.

    Why it never feels expensive

    Between 2020 and 2022 the price of a US disposable rose from $8.49 to $14.07 — while the price of the liquid inside fell 69%, from $7.96 to $2.45 a millilitre. Each device costs more and holds far more. You are not imagining that it feels like the same spend. You are getting an excellent deal on a larger quantity of the thing you would rather use less of.

    69% Fall in the price per millilitre of e-liquid in US disposables, 2020–2022, while the sticker price rose 66%. Tobacco Control, PMID 37536928
